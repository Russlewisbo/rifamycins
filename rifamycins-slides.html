Learning Objectives


  • Explain how rifamycins inhibit bacterial RNA polymerase and why a single rpoB mutation matters clinically
  • Compare the pharmacokinetics and CYP induction of the four agents
  • Apply the rifamycins across TB, LTBI, NTM, leprosy, and staphylococcal/device infection
  • Decide when to add rifampin — and when not to
  • Position rifaximin among gut-selective indications

Introduction & History

A drug named after a crime film




  • Isolated by Sensi and colleagues, Milan, 1959 — from Amycolatopsis mediterranei
  • Nicknamed “Rififi” after a popular French heist film → the class name “rifamycins”
  • Rifamycin SV used clinically in 1963; replaced by rifampin (1968) for better bioavailability and broader activity, especially vs M. tuberculosis

Rifampin displaced the parent compound because of pharmacokinetics — a recurring theme in this class, where each newer agent is essentially a PK story (Forrest and Tamura, 2010; Sensi, 1983).

The four approved rifamycins


  • Rifampin — systemic workhorse; TB, staph/biofilm
  • Rifabutin — long half-life, weaker CYP induction; preferred with HIV protease inhibitors
  • Rifapentine — long half-life; enables once-weekly / short-course LTBI
  • Rifaximin — negligibly absorbed, gut-selective; HE, travelers’ diarrhea, IBS

One pharmacophore with four PK personalities. Everything downstream — dosing, interactions, indications — falls out of absorption, half-life, and enzyme induction. LTBI-Latent tuberculosis infection; HE- hepatic encephalopathy

Mechanism & Resistance

Ansamycin structure


  • Ansamycin family (Latin ansa, “handle”): aliphatic bridge across a naphthoquinone core
  • Rifampin = 3-(4-methyl-1-piperazinyl)-iminomethyl derivative of rifamycin SV
  • Small structural tweaks → large PK differences across the four agents

Structures of the four major rifamycins.(A) Rifampin. (B) Rifabutin. (C) Rifapentine. (D) Rifaximin

Mechanism: Inhibition of RNA polymerase


(Artsimovitch et al., 2005; Campbell et al., 2001)

Why the therapeutic index is favorable?


  • Eukaryotic RNA polymerases do not bind rifamycins
  • Human mitochondrial RNAP is insensitive at therapeutic concentrations
  • Selective toxicity despite targeting a fundamental cellular process

Resistance: the rpoB problem


  • Spontaneous resistance at 10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ per cell division

  • Mutations almost exclusively in rpoB → reduced RNAP affinity

    95% of resistant M. tuberculosis map to the 81-bp RRDR (codons 507–533)

  • Hot spots: codons 526 and 531 → high-level, cross-class resistance

Cross-resistance across rifampin/rifabutin/rifapentine at codons 526/531 is clinically important: you cannot rescue a high-level rifampin-resistant strain by switching to another rifamycin (Ohno et al., 1996; Ramaswamy and Musser, 1998).

Alternative resistance mechanisms


  • Impaired uptake — Pseudomonas fluorescens
  • ADP-ribosylation via arr gene — M. smegmatis; plasmid-borne arr-2 in Enterobacterales, P. aeruginosa
  • Intrinsically less-susceptible enzyme — Nocardia

These explain intrinsic gram-negative resistance and why rifampin MICs for Enterobacterales are high — relevant when people propose rifampin “synergy” for MDR gram-negatives (Floss and Yu, 2005).

Clinical consequence: NEVER MONOTHERAPY!


  • One point mutation → high-level resistance

  • High bacterial burden (cavitary TB, abscess, endocarditis, biofilm) guarantees a resistant subpopulation

  • Rule: rifamycins are always given in combination

Rapid molecular detection: Xpert MTB/RIF


  • Heminested PCR probes the RRDR of rpoB
  • Detects M. tuberculosis and rifampin resistance in ~2 hours
  • WHO-recommended, including extrapulmonary disease
  • Confirm genotypic resistance with sequencing/culture (≥10% discrepant results)

Because rifampin resistance is a surrogate for MDR-TB, Xpert effectively screens for MDR at point of care — one of the highest-impact diagnostics in global TB. Note the false-positive caveat in low-prevalence settings (Drobniewski et al., 2015; Helb et al., 2010; Steingart et al., 2014).

Pharmacology & Interactions

Comparative pharmacokinetics



Drug Bioavail. (%) t½ (hr) Protein binding
Rifampin 68 2–5 80%
Rifabutin 20 32–67 72–85%
Rifapentine 70 (food) 14–18 98%
Rifaximin <0.4 1.8–4.8 62–68%

(Blaschke and Skinner, 1996; Burman et al., 2001)

Half-life is the story: rifampin daily, rifabutin daily but forgiving, rifapentine weekly, rifaximin acts locally. Rifapentine must be taken with food (50% higher Cmax with a high-fat meal).

Autoinduction


  • Rifampin and rifabutin induce their own metabolism
  • Repeated dosing ↓ AUC over the first ~1–2 weeks
  • Long post-antibiotic effect vs M. tuberculosis (68–75 h) for the systemic agents

Autoinduction matters for TDM interpretation and for why steady-state exposures are lower than a single-dose PK study would predict (Burman et al., 2001).

CYP3A4 induction — the core interaction


  • Rifampin: most potent inducer of CYP3A4 (also 1A2, 2C, 2D6, P-glycoprotein)
  • Maximal at ~1 week; resolves ~2 weeks after stopping
  • Lowers AUC/Cmax of many co-administered drugs
  • Induction rank: rifampin > rifapentine > rifabutin

The induction-potency rank is the single most useful interaction fact — it is the entire rationale for substituting rifabutin in ART (Baciewicz et al., 2013).

High-yield interacting drugs (1 of 2)


  • Anticoagulants: warfarin, DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran)
  • Antimicrobials: azoles, clarithromycin, linezolid, HIV PIs/NNRTIs/integrase inhibitors, HCV DAAs, nirmatrelvir/ritonavir
  • Immunosuppressants: cyclosporine, tacrolimus, sirolimus

Transplant and HIV are the two services that page you about rifampin. Flag tacrolimus/cyclosporine and the antiretrovirals as the ones that cause real harm through subtherapeutic exposure (Baciewicz et al., 2013; Panel on Antiretroviral Guidelines for Adults and Adolescents, 2023).

High-yield interacting drugs (2 of 2)


  • Endocrine: oral contraceptives (counsel on failure), glucocorticoids, sulfonylureas, levothyroxine
  • Cardiovascular: calcium channel blockers, digoxin, quinidine
  • CNS/analgesia: methadone (withdrawal risk), phenytoin, benzodiazepines

Two counseling points patients actually experience: contraceptive failure and methadone withdrawal. Both are common board and ward scenarios (Baciewicz et al., 2013).

Managing interactions in practice


  • Can’t tolerate strong induction (e.g., HIV PI regimen)? → switch to rifabutin, dose-adjusted
  • Warn: red-orange urine, tears, sweat; permanent staining of soft contact lenses
  • Verify contraceptive backup and re-dose narrow-therapeutic-index drugs

The contact-lens and body-fluid discoloration point reassures patients and improves adherence. The rifabutin substitution is the actionable clinical move.

The Four Agents

Rifampin — pharmacokinetics


  • 600 mg daily (10–20 mg/kg children); only rifamycin available IV
  • Rapid, complete absorption — better on an empty stomach
  • Wide distribution incl. CSF and bone; crosses placenta / breast milk
  • Biliary elimination; no renal dose adjustment; adjust in severe hepatic impairment

“IV available” and “no renal adjustment” are the two facts that come up on rounds. CSF penetration underpins its use in some CNS and shunt infections (Burman et al., 2001).

Rifampin — flu-like syndrome & renal toxicity


  • Flu-like syndrome (fever, myalgia, headache): immune-complex mediated, worse with intermittent dosing → switch to daily
  • Acute interstitial nephritis with antirifampin antibodies — usually reversible on stopping
  • Immune-mediated thrombocytopenia/hemolysis; rare DIC

The pattern to teach: rifampin’s immune-mediated reactions cluster with intermittent/re-exposure dosing. Daily dosing is protective — counterintuitive but important (De Vriese et al., 1998; Martinez et al., 1999).

Rifampin — hepatotoxicity


  • Usually mild, cholestatic; benign early hyperbilirubinemia
  • Monotherapy hepatotoxicity ~1.1%; rises with isoniazid
  • Rifampin + pyrazinamide for LTBI → severe hepatitis: abandoned
  • SLCO1B1 *15 haplotype predisposes

The RIF+PZA LTBI story is a cautionary tale worth telling: an attractive short regimen abandoned after fatal liver injury. It also motivates the modern rifapentine-based short regimens (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2001; Li et al., 2012; Yew and Leung, 2006).

Rifampin — antimicrobial spectrum


  • Most potent vs gram-positives: S. aureus, CoNS, streptococci, Listeria, C. difficile
  • Intracellular pathogens: Chlamydia, Legionella, Brucella, Bartonella
  • Mycobacteria: M. tuberculosis, MAC, M. kansasii, M. marinum
  • Gram-negatives: intrinsically high MICs

Contrast the sub-0.015 µg/mL staphylococcal MICs with the ≥8–32 µg/mL Enterobacterales MICs on the next slide — that four-log gap explains the entire clinical positioning (Thornsberry et al., 1983).

Exquisite gram-positive potency, poor gram-negative activity. This is why rifampin is a staph/mycobacterial drug, not a gram-negative one (Thornsberry et al., 1983).

Rifabutin


  • Spiropiperidyl analogue; long half-life (~35 h), once daily
  • Weaker CYP3A4 inducer than rifampin → preferred with HIV protease inhibitors
  • Dose ↓ to 150 mg with ritonavir-boosted PI; ↑ to 450–600 mg with efavirenz
  • Reduce dose if CrCl <30 mL/min

The bidirectional dosing (down with PIs, up with efavirenz) trips people up. PIs raise rifabutin (lower the dose); efavirenz lowers rifabutin (raise the dose) (Blaschke and Skinner, 1996; Burman et al., 2001).

Rifabutin — distinctive toxicity


  • Uveitis (dose- and interaction-dependent; may mimic endophthalmitis with hypopyon)
  • Leukopenia, polyarthralgias
  • Active vs M. tuberculosis, MAC, H. pylori (rescue therapy)

Uveitis with clarithromycin or fluconazole co-administration (which raise rifabutin levels) is the classic exam association — anterior chamber, responds to steroids/cycloplegics (Graham et al., 2020; Griffith et al., 1995).

Rifapentine


  • Cyclopentyl rifamycin; more potent vs M. tuberculosis (MIC 0.06 vs 0.25)
  • Long half-life (14–18 h) → once-weekly / short-course regimens
  • Take with food; induces CYP3A4/2C
  • Hyperuricemia up to 21%; activity essentially limited to M. tuberculosis

Rifapentine exists to solve the adherence problem of long TB therapy. Its narrow spectrum (basically TB only) means you will not see it outside mycobacterial disease (Heifets et al., 1990; Jarvis and Lamb, 1998).

Rifaximin


  • Extra C-3/C-4 ring; <0.01% absorbed, ~97% recovered in stool
  • Very high fecal concentrations (~8000 µg/mL); negligible systemic interactions
  • Active vs enteric pathogens + C. difficile; modulates microbiome / NF-κB
  • No dose adjustment in cirrhosis

Rifaximin breaks the CYP-interaction rule precisely because it never reaches the systemic circulation. That is the whole design. Keep it mentally separate from the systemic three (DuPont, 2011; Koo and DuPont, 2010).

Tuberculosis & NTM

Rifampin: cornerstone of TB therapy


  • Adding rifampin cut TB treatment from 18 → 9 months
  • Best sterilizing activity among first-line drugs (kills dormant persisters)
  • Penetrates cavitary lesions; cheap, globally available, safe
  • First-line rifamycin for TB

Sterilizing activity — killing the slowly/non-replicating persisters — is the property that shortens therapy and prevents relapse. It is distinct from early bactericidal activity (Alfarisi et al., 2017; Goble et al., 1993).

Is 10 mg/kg the right dose?


  • Killing is concentration-dependent, best predicted by AUC/MIC
  • 10 mg/kg sits at the low end of a steep dose-response curve
  • Higher doses (up to 35 mg/kg) safe; faster culture conversion
  • Prospect: shorter regimens with optimized dosing

This is an active area — the historical dose was set by cost and PK assumptions from the 1970s, not efficacy. High-dose rifampin trials (PanACEA, etc.) are reshaping practice(Alfarisi et al., 2017; Goble et al., 1993).

TB dosing: an unsettled debate


  • Original 10 mg/kg reflected 1950s–70s manufacturing cost and supply, not a defined efficacy ceiling
  • Doses to 35–50 mg/kg tolerated in trials; hyperbilirubinemia (without frank DILI) emerges near 50 mg/kg
  • Phase 3 RIFASHORT (40 mg/kg) did not meet noninferiority vs. standard care overall, though favorable-prognosis subgroups performed reasonably
  • WHO is actively reconsidering dosing guidance — experts disagree on whether 1200 mg should become the new starting dose

Frame this as genuinely unresolved rather than settled. Some recommend a 1200 mg starting dose to avoid the low-exposure tail; while others emphasize that per-kg toxicity data above ~20 mg/kg remain uncertain (Jindani et al., 2023).

Special populations: when to push the dose


  • TB meningitis: rifampin CSF penetration is poor relative to plasma. The 2025 HARVEST trial (499 adults, Indonesia/South Africa/Uganda) found high-dose oral rifampicin (35 mg/kg) did not improve 6-month survival vs. standard 10 mg/kg (44.6% vs. 40.7% mortality; HR 1.17, 95% CI 0.89–1.54) — this supersedes the podcast’s “inconclusive, still under study” framing
  • MAC: MIC distribution is right-shifted (~2 dilutions above M. tuberculosis) — standard TB doses may be inadequate, though this is not yet a guideline-endorsed practice
  • Malabsorption (bariatric surgery, interacting drugs) → unexpectedly low levels; TDM (e.g., paired 2 h/6 h or 3 h/7 h sampling) can detect and guide correction
  • Gradual dose escalation is favored by some experts in older/NTM patients with tolerability concerns — an individualized, not standardized, approach

See reference (Meya et al., 2025) for TB meningitis dosing. MAC/malabsorption/escalation points reflect expert practice patterns not a validated algorithm — TDM targets and escalation thresholds vary by center. Useful bridge into “trust but verify” as a TDM philosophy.

Rifapentine in active TB


  • Once-weekly rifapentine–isoniazid (continuation phase): relapse + acquired rifamycin monoresistance in HIV
  • → Do not use intermittent rifapentine in HIV
  • Higher-dose daily rifapentine under study to shorten therapy

The monoresistance signal in HIV-positive patients on intermittent rifapentine is a key safety lesson and the reason intermittent dosing is contraindicated in that group (Benator et al., 2002; Dooley et al., 2012; Jindani et al., 2014).

Rifabutin in TB with HIV


  • Efficacy similar to rifampin
  • Weaker enzyme induction → preferred with protease-inhibitor ART
  • Rifampin remains preferred with efavirenz-based ART

The decision rule: PI-based ART → rifabutin; efavirenz-based ART → rifampin. This maps directly onto the CYP induction hierarchy taught earlier (Baciewicz et al., 2013; Burman et al., 2001).

LTBI: shorter rifamycin regimens


  • 4 months daily rifampin
  • 3–4 months daily isoniazid + rifampin
  • 12 weeks weekly isoniazid + rifapentine (3HP)
  • Shorter regimens → better completion, less hepatotoxicity than 9 months INH

The field has decisively moved away from 9 months of isoniazid. Completion rate — not just efficacy — is the metric that changed practice (Pease et al., 2017).

3HP — weekly rifapentine + isoniazid


  • 12 once-weekly directly-observed doses
  • As effective as 9 months daily isoniazid
  • Higher completion, fewer severe adverse events
  • Now an equal alternative for healthy patients ≥12 y

The PREVENT TB trial is the landmark. Rifapentine’s long half-life is what makes weekly dosing pharmacologically possible (Sterling et al., 2011).

1HP — one month for people with HIV


  • Daily rifapentine + isoniazid for 1 month
  • Noninferior to 9 months isoniazid in HIV
  • Higher completion (97% vs 90%)

The BRIEF-TB / A5279 trial (Swindells, NEJM 2019). For a population where adherence is the central obstacle, one month is transformative (Swindells et al., 2019).

Mycobacterium avium complex


  • Pulmonary MAC: macrolide + rifampin (or rifabutin) + ethambutol
  • Rifabutin favored with significant drug interactions
  • Disseminated AIDS-MAC: adding rifabutin improved survival, fewer relapses

Note the rifampin–macrolide interaction lowers macrolide levels — a real pharmacologic tension in the recommended regimen. Rifabutin dosing must account for the macrolide (Benson et al., 2003; Griffith et al., 2007).

M. kansasii and leprosy


  • M. kansasii: rifampin + isoniazid + ethambutol; excellent response, low relapse

  • Leprosy: rifampin rapidly bactericidal vs M. leprae

    • dapsone (paucibacillary); + dapsone + clofazimine (multibacillary)

    • single-dose rifampin reduces incidence in contacts — never monotherapy

Leprosy reinforces the monotherapy rule in a completely different organism — a nice way to generalize the rpoB lesson beyond TB and staph (Bullock, 1983; Griffith et al., 2007; Moet et al., 2008).

Staphylococcal & Device Infections

Why rifampin is attractive against staph


These four properties are exactly what you want against a foreign body (Costerton et al., 1999; Mandell, 1983).

…but monotherapy fails


  • High-burden staph (abscess, endocarditis, biofilm) selects resistance rapidly
  • Rifampin is always a combination agent for staph
  • The clinical question is never “rifampin?” but “rifampin, with what, and when?”

“Should I add rifampin?” The better question embeds combination, timing (after source control), and susceptibility (Sande and Johnson, 1975)

In-vitro synergy testing — read with caution


  • Time-kill and checkerboard/disk-diffusion synergy methods are not CLSI-standardized; explicitly described as “not reproducible” and “method dependent” even in reference texts
  • In-vitro synergy does not reliably predict in-vivo effect, and can even reverse (antagonism in vivo despite synergy in vitro, or the converse)
  • No randomized controlled trial has shown that synergy testing predicts clinical outcome for staphylococcal infections
  • The mechanistic case for rifampin (biofilm penetration, activity vs. dormant organisms) is attractive but should not be over-interpreted as bedside-validated

Many clinicians feel that used of rifampin combinations in this area is “more religion than science.” (Doern, 2014) is the standard peer-reviewed review of these methodological limitations

Native valve endocarditis & uncomplicated bacteremia


  • Adding rifampin does not improve outcomes
  • Selects resistance; potential harm
  • IDSA / AHA: recommend against rifampin in staphylococcal NVE

This is where trainees most often over-reach. Make the “against” recommendation explicit and tie it to resistance selection in a high-burden, no-hardware setting (Baddour et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2011).

S. aureus bacteremia — the evidence


  • Surgically treated endocarditis: adjunctive rifampin → no survival/reoperation benefit
  • Meta-analysis: insufficient evidence for adjunctive rifampin in SAB
  • Retrospective signal of benefit only with a deep focus

The ARREST trial reinforced no benefit and more interactions/resistance. The honest summary: outside retained hardware, the data do not support routine rifampin in SAB (Forsblom et al., 2015; Ma et al., 2020; Shrestha et al., 2016).

Prosthetic valve endocarditis


  • Staphylococcal PVE: rifampin part of triple therapy
  • e.g., vancomycin + rifampin + gentamicin
  • Rationale: prosthetic material = biofilm

PVE is the endocarditis setting where guidelines do include rifampin — the difference from NVE is the prosthetic (biofilm) surface. Start rifampin after bacteremia clears (Baddour et al., 2015; Karchmer et al., 1983).

PVE rifampin — a fraying evidence base


  • The AHA/IDSA recommendation traces to a single observational study from roughly 30 years ago — never confirmed by an RCT
  • A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of the 4 available studies found no clinical benefit of adjunctive rifampin (or gentamicin) in staphylococcal PVE, and called for the guideline recommendation to be reevaluated pending an RCT
  • Practicing clinicians describe using it “with little conviction,” readily substituting another approach when tolerability or interactions provide a reason to

A guideline recommendation that has outlived its evidence base — useful contrast with the stronger device-retention data on the next slide (Zimmerli). (Ryder et al., 2022).

Landmark: rifampin in orthopedic device infection


  • Zimmerli FBI Study Group RCT (JAMA 1998)
  • Ciprofloxacin ± rifampin for stable implant-related staph infection, debridement + retention
  • Rifampin arm: markedly higher cure
  • Established rifampin’s genuine niche: biofilm on retained hardware

This is the positive counterpoint to the endocarditis slides — the one high-quality RCT showing rifampin combination works, and it is specifically device-associated infection managed with retention (Zimmerli et al., 1998).

PJI — cohorts, duration, and nuance


  • Cohorts support rifampin combinations in PJI with debridement + implant retention
  • Randomized data on levofloxacin + rifampin duration
  • Large multicentre study: refined if, when, how to add rifampin in acute PJI

The Beldman 2021 study is worth flagging: it tempers enthusiasm, showing timing and companion-drug choice matter and that indiscriminate early rifampin can harm. Nuance, not reflex (Beldman et al., 2021; El Helou et al., 2010; Lora-Tamayo et al., 2016; Peel et al., 2013).

When to add rifampin — and when not to


  • Add: retained prosthetic material / biofilm (PJI with retention, PVE), susceptible isolate, after source control and bacteremia clearance
  • Avoid: uncomplicated SAB, native valve endocarditis
  • Never: start into a high-burden bacteremia before source control

The timing rule — wait for blood cultures to clear before adding rifampin — prevents the resistance that ruins the drug.

Bone & joint infection: dosing and companion drugs


  • When used, experts favor simple 600 mg once daily — achieves AUC/MIC ~800–900 against typical staphylococcal MICs; higher per-kg TB-style dosing is not well tolerated in this setting
  • The only companion regimen with real trial support is a fluoroquinolone + rifampin (the Zimmerli paradigm); most other pairings are extrapolated (“manufactured”) rather than trial-proven
  • Doxycycline + rifampin is used in practice but lacks controlled-trial evidence, and rifampin’s induction of doxycycline clearance is of uncertain clinical significance
  • EVRIOS (multicentre French RCT, 543 patients): daily rifampin 10 mg/kg was noninferior to 20 mg/kg for staphylococcal bone/joint infection, with more severe adverse events in the higher-dose arm — direct trial support for the “simple 600 mg-range” approach
  • Reserve for high relapse-risk patients without prohibitive interactions — not a default add-on

Useful to contrast “evidence-backed” (quinolone+rifampin) against “plausible but unproven” (doxycycline+rifampin) companion regimens — a distinction trainees often miss (Arvieux et al., 2025).

Rifabutin as a rifampin substitute?


  • Same RNA-polymerase mechanism, weaker CYP3A4 induction → fewer interactions (e.g., methadone, anticoagulants)
  • Retrospective single-center study (87 patients meeting IDSA rifampin criteria for staph PJI): completing rifampin improved treatment success (OR 0.76 for failure without it, p=0.031) and lowered revision risk (HR 2.22 without it, p=0.035)
  • Of those same patients, 18.4% had a drug interaction that contraindicated rifampin, vs. only 3.4% for rifabutin — rifabutin substitution preserves access to combination therapy for most of that group
  • Still only one center’s retrospective data — not a head-to-head rifampin-vs-rifabutin efficacy trial

Upgraded from anecdote to actual retrospective comparative data — a good example of a plausible clinical workaround now with some evidentiary support, though still short of a trial. (Leal et al., 2025).

VISA/VRSA and rpoB


  • rpoB mutations reducing vancomycin susceptibility often confer rifampin resistance
  • Links vancomycin exposure ↔︎ rifampin resistance in S. aureus
  • Rifampin active vs 92% of VRSA but only 51% of VISA in one series

A mechanistic curiosity with practical weight: the same cell-wall-thickening/rpoB biology couples the two resistances, so don’t assume rifampin susceptibility in reduced-vancomycin-susceptibility strains (Watanabe et al., 2011).

Prevention: chemoprophylaxis & devices


  • Meningococcal contacts: rifampin 600 mg BID ×2 d (resistance emerges — ceftriaxone/cipro often preferred)
  • H. influenzae type b contacts: rifampin ×4 d
  • CATCH trial: minocycline-rifampin catheters ↓ bloodstream infection 57%
  • WRAP-IT: antibacterial envelope ↓ major CIED infection (HR 0.60)

Device prevention is a growing, evidence-backed use — WRAP-IT (Tarakji, NEJM 2019) is a large RCT. Contrast with the shakier therapeutic data, and note resistance concerns limit prophylactic systemic use (Gilbert et al., 2016; Tarakji et al., 2019).

Rifaximin & GI Indications

Rifaximin: gut-selective rifamycin


  • Negligible absorption → acts in the lumen
  • Active vs enteric pathogens and C. difficile
  • Microbiome modulation + anti-inflammatory (NF-κB)
  • Few systemic interactions — reserve for GI indications

Set up the indication slides: everything rifaximin does well is downstream of “it stays in the gut.” That is both its strength and the boundary of its usefulness (DuPont, 2011; Koo and DuPont, 2010).

Hepatic encephalopathy


  • Rifaximin 550 mg BID — approved for prevention
  • Fewer breakthrough HE episodes; ↓ HE-related hospitalization vs placebo
  • ≥ as effective as lactulose, better tolerated

The Bass 2010 NEJM trial is the pivotal evidence. Mechanism: fewer ammonia-producing gut organisms. Usually added to lactulose rather than replacing it (Bass et al., 2010; Paik et al., 2005).

Travelers’ diarrhea


  • Shortens time to symptom resolution (non-invasive pathogens)
  • ~72% effective for chemoprophylaxis
  • Fluoroquinolones preferred for prophylaxis (broader, invasive coverage)

Rifaximin treats/prevents non-invasive (ETEC) diarrhea; it does not cover invasive/febrile dysentery, which is the limitation clinicians must remember (DuPont et al., 2005).

Recurrent C. difficile infection


  • “Rifaximin chaser” after vancomycin for recurrent CDI (response up to 83%)
  • Resistance is a real concern — high MICs, disproportionately BI/NAP1/027
  • Not first-line; a niche adjunct

Small studies, resistance risk. In the fidaxomicin/FMT era, rifaximin is a limited option, but residents will still see it used as a chaser (DuPont et al., 2005).

IBS and inflammatory bowel disease


  • FDA-approved for IBS without constipation (modest benefit)
  • Possible steroid-sparing role in IBD (UC, Crohn, pouchitis)
  • Benefit attributed to microbiome + anti-inflammatory effects

IBS is the highest-volume real-world use of rifaximin. The IBD data are softer — present as emerging rather than established (DuPont et al., 2005).

Rifaximin resistance & the microbiome


  • Rifampin-resistant staphylococci recovered from skin post-therapy (persist ≤9 wk)
  • Rifaximin-resistant E. coli selected in the gut
  • Reserve empirical/repeat courses

Even a “non-systemic” rifamycin selects class resistance. This closes the loop with the rpoB theme: no rifamycin is exempt from the single-mutation problem.

Future Directions & Summary

Rifamycins in development


  • Aerosolized rifampin (dry powders, liposomes, + colistin) for pulmonary delivery
  • Rifamycin-eluting orthopedic scaffolds / PMMA
  • Colon-targeted rifamycin SV MMX
  • Multi-targeting conjugates to beat single-mutation resistance: TNP-2092, TNP-2198

The conjugates are the conceptually interesting frontier: by hitting RNAP plus DNA gyrase/topoisomerase (TNP-2092) or adding a nitroimidazole (TNP-2198), they raise the genetic barrier that a single rpoB mutation otherwise lowers. Full citations for these two flagged for verification (Ma et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2020).

Key take-home points


  1. Inhibit prokaryotic RNAP (rpoB); one mutation → high-level resistance → never monotherapy

  2. CYP3A4 induction: rifampin > rifapentine > rifabutin; rifabutin is the ART workaround

  3. TB: rifampin best sterilizer; rifapentine enables 3HP/1HP; rifabutin with PIs

  4. Staph: rifampin’s niche is retained hardware/biofilm — add after source control; avoid in uncomplicated SAB/NVE

  5. Rifaximin = gut-selective: HE, travelers’ diarrhea, IBS, recurrent CDI
